# El Niño 2026–27 map: literature check for four proposed Chile / Altiplano additions

Prepared 2026-09-24. Context: record, east-Pacific-leaning El Niño peaking ~Nov–Dec 2026; positive IOD developing; PDO strongly negative (~−1.8). Year 0 = 2026 (developing), year +1 = 2027 (decay). A Chilean colleague proposed four additions; each is assessed below.

**How things were verified**
- Every DOI below was resolved through the Crossref API (`https://api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>`), and title, authors and year were checked against the citation. One exception is flagged: the DOI printed on the SciELO page for Vargas et al. (2000) is **not registered** (Crossref and doi.org both return 404), so that paper is cited by its SciELO URL.
- Quote tags:
  - **[VERIFIED-PRIMARY: URL]**: read verbatim in the full text on the publisher or repository page.
  - **[ABSTRACT-ONLY]**: verbatim abstract text from the publisher-deposited Crossref record or the OpenAlex record. Full text not read.
  - **[SECONDARY/UNVERIFIED]**: news, conference abstracts, Wikipedia or search-result text that I did not check against a peer-reviewed primary source.
- ONI values are taken from the local file `oni_cpc_2026-09-15.txt` (CPC ONI v5). "Strong" El Niño means peak ONI ≥ 1.5 between JAS(0) and DJF(+1). By that rule the strong events are 1957, 1965, 1972, 1982, 1991, 1997, 2009, 2015 and 2023 (1987 peaks at 1.49 and is left out).
- Access limits: full text was **not** read for Garreaud, Vuille & Clement (2003), Garreaud et al. (2009, *Palaeo3*), Houston (2006), Vuille & Ammann (1997), Garreaud (2018, *Clim. Res.*; the publisher page served a bot check, which I did not try to bypass) or Garreaud et al. (2020, *IJC*). Claims from these papers are abstract-level only.

**The most important timing finding.** For central Chile and the Atacama, the peer-reviewed El Niño wet signal is concurrent with the developing year: winter (JJA) and late spring (Oct–Nov) of year 0. It is not in the autumn–winter of the decay year. Montecinos et al. (2000) found the ENSO–rainfall relation in fall "relatively weak". Montecinos & Aceituno (2003) state that central Chile rainfall "is not significantly related to ENSO in fall". Decay-year winters (1973, 1998, 2010) often turned dry as La Niña developed. For the 2026–27 map, the only still-actionable wet signal in Chile is **Oct–Nov 2026 at ~35–38°S**. "Wetter Mar–Sep 2027" would misplace the season.

## 1. Altiplano: dry Dec–Mar 2026–27

**Sign, season, mechanism.** Dry during the DJF (to March) rainy season of the mature El Niño. The mechanism is well established. El Niño warms the tropical troposphere, which steepens the meridional temperature gradient and strengthens the upper-level westerlies over the central Andes. Stronger westerlies mean fewer days of easterly flow, which is what carries Amazon moisture up to the plateau.

**Key papers and quotes**
1. Vuille, M., Bradley, R.S. & Keimig, F. (2000). Interannual climate variability in the Central Andes and its relation to tropical Pacific and Atlantic forcing. *JGR-Atmospheres* 105(D10), 12447–12460. https://doi.org/10.1029/2000JD900134
   - "DJF precipitation is also primarily related to ENSO, featuring below (above) average precipitation during El Niño (La Niña). Precipitation over the dry western part of the Altiplano shows the closest relationship with ENSO" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
   - "Over the northern Altiplano the precipitation signal is reversed in the austral summer following the peak phase of ENSO" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. (Which summer "following" means is ambiguous in the abstract. I read it as the next summer, i.e., DJF 2027–28, but I could not confirm this in the full text.)
2. Garreaud, R. & Aceituno, P. (2001). Interannual rainfall variability over the South American Altiplano. *J. Climate* 14, 2779–2789. https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0442(2001)014%3C2779:IRVOTS%3E2.0.CO;2
   - "the association between El Niño (La Niña) and drier (rainier) than normal summers on the Altiplano can be explained by ENSO-related warming (cooling) of the tropical troposphere" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/clim/14/12/1520-0442_2001_014_2779_irvots_2.0.co_2.xml]
   - Caveat, same source: "neither all rainiest/driest summers occur during extreme ENSO years, nor all El Niño/La Niña years are associated with extreme rainfall anomalies" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
   - Documented failure: January 1973, in the mature stage of the 1972–73 El Niño, had "Altiplano rainfall slightly above normal". In that case the warming extended into the subtropics, so the westerly jet strengthened at 30–40°S instead of over the Andes [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
   - "The amount (and even the sign) of the Altiplano rainfall anomalies is, however, highly dependent on the location of zonal wind anomalies." [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
3. Vuille, M. (1999). Atmospheric circulation over the Bolivian Altiplano during dry and wet periods and extreme phases of the Southern Oscillation. *Int. J. Climatol.* 19, 1579–1600. https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-0088(19991130)19:14%3C1579::AID-JOC441%3E3.0.CO;2-N
   - "Precipitation tends to be deficient over the western Bolivian Altiplano during LOW index summers ... but the relation is weak and statistically insignificant." [ABSTRACT-ONLY] (LOW = negative SOI, i.e., El Niño.)
4. Sulca, J., Takahashi, K., Espinoza, J.-C., Vuille, M. & Lavado-Casimiro, W. (2018; online 2017). Impacts of different ENSO flavors and tropical Pacific convection variability (ITCZ, SPCZ) on austral summer rainfall in South America, with a focus on Peru. *Int. J. Climatol.* 38, 420–435. https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.5185
   - "positive E enhances wet conditions along the coasts of Ecuador and northern Peru ... and induces dry conditions in Altiplano" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. This matters for an **east-Pacific-leaning** 2026 event. Central-Pacific warming ("positive C") also dries the tropical Andes, so both flavours point dry.
5. Lavado-Casimiro, W. & Espinoza, J.C. (2014). Impactos de El Niño y La Niña en las lluvias del Perú (1965–2007). *Rev. Bras. Meteorol.* 29, 171–182. https://doi.org/10.1590/S0102-77862014000200003
   - The Titicaca basin "presenta un importante déficit de lluvias durante ENF" (strong El Niño). The leading mode is tied to 1983 and 1998, which produced "sequías en la VT" (the Titicaca basin) [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
6. Imfeld, N. et al. (2019). Summertime precipitation deficits in the southern Peruvian highlands since 1964. *Int. J. Climatol.* 39, 4497–4513. https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.6087
   - "El Niño is not a sufficient stratification criterion for precipitation deficits during the rainy season (JFM) in the southern Peruvian highlands ... not all El Niño events produce these westerly wind anomalies." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
7. Garreaud, R.D. (2009). The Andes climate and weather. *Adv. Geosci.* 22, 3–11. https://doi.org/10.5194/adgeo-22-3-2009
   - "El Niño (La Niña) summers also bring less (more) precipitation to the Altiplano ... although the ENSO-rainfall relationship in this region is not too robust." [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://adgeo.copernicus.org/articles/22/3/2009/adgeo-22-3-2009.pdf]
8. Aceituno, P. & Garreaud, R. (2025). The Climate of the South American Altiplano. *Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Climate Science*. https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780190228620.013.977
   - "the Altiplano tends to experience below (above) average precipitation during El Niño (La Niña) summers because of the stronger (weaker) westerly winds in those periods." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
9. Garreaud, R., Vuille, M. & Clement, A.C. (2003). The climate of the Altiplano: observed current conditions and mechanisms of past changes. *Palaeo3* 194, 5–22. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0031-0182(03)00269-4. DOI verified; **full text and abstract not accessed**, so nothing is quoted.

**Behaviour in strong events**
- **1982–83:** severe drought. Canedo-Rosso et al. (2021, *NHESS* 21, 995–1010, https://doi.org/10.5194/nhess-21-995-2021): "The extreme drought of 1982–1983 coincided with a very strong El Niño phase. For this event, the largest economic losses caused by droughts during the study period are reported" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/21/995/2021/nhess-21-995-2021.pdf]. Gutierrez-Villarreal et al. (2024) also call it "the El Niño-related drought of 1982-83" [ABSTRACT-ONLY].
- **1997–98:** drought in the Bolivian Altiplano, "the very strong El Niño phase of 1997–1998, which reported the second largest economic losses" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, Canedo-Rosso et al. 2021]. It was a major drought by the temperature-based index (TCI) but was not among the major events by the vegetation index (VCI or VHI), so severity depends on the metric.
- **1972–73:** January 1973 was slightly wet (see Garreaud & Aceituno 2001 above). This is a documented failure.
- **1991–92** (moderate-strong): "showed low severity (mild drought VCI ≤ 40 %), and no economic losses were reported" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, Canedo-Rosso et al. 2021].
- **2015–16:** **not well documented in anything I could verify.** Do not use Lake Poopó drying as evidence. Satgé et al. (2017, *Remote Sens.* 9, 218, https://doi.org/10.3390/rs9030218) link the 2015 Poopó drought to a "positive annual rainfall anomaly in 2015" and point to agriculture and evapotranspiration [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. Search results claiming DJF 2016 deficits in Puno are [SECONDARY/UNVERIFIED].
- **2023–24:** the historic 2022–23 Titicaca drought came **before** El Niño and was tied to **La Niña** during the pre-wet season (Oct–Dec). Gutierrez-Villarreal et al. (2024, *WACE* 45, 100710, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wace.2024.100710) call this "a new atmospheric mechanism related to extreme droughts in the TDPS associated with La Niña SST anomalies during the pre-wet season. This goes beyond the traditional understanding of El Niño events, especially the strongest ones, being associated with dry conditions in the TDPS during the wet season (December–March)." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. Sulca et al. (2025) list a Titicaca lake-level minimum starting February 2023 and lasting more than 9 months [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2025.1564040/full]. I found no verified peer-reviewed statement on DJF 2023–24 Altiplano rainfall itself.
- **1957, 1965:** not checked.

**PDO caveat (unresolved).** The literature disagrees:
- Sulca et al. (2025) cite prior work that Altiplano summer drying is stronger when "the IPO warm phase and El Niño" coincide [VERIFIED-PRIMARY, citing Dong et al. 2018; Canedo-Rosso et al. 2021]. That would imply a **weaker** drying under today's strongly negative PDO.
- Angulo & Pereira Filho (2023, *Atmosphere* 14, 1233, https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos14081233) instead report that "The negative phase of the IPO aligns with extreme drought periods" [ABSTRACT-ONLY].
- Sulca et al. (2025) conclude that PDO/IPO "do not drive long-lived LTWL minima events because their relationship does not remain consistent over time" [ABSTRACT-ONLY].

I treat the PDO as a source of added uncertainty, not a directional adjustment.

**Recommendation: medium.** Map as "drier than normal Dec 2026–Mar 2027", focused on the western and southern Altiplano (SW Bolivia, the Chilean Altiplano, Arica–Parinacota/Tarapacá/Antofagasta highlands, and the Puno–Oruro–Potosí region). Say that the north (Titicaca) is less reliable. The EP-leaning flavour supports the call (Sulca et al. 2018). Well-documented failures (1973, and 1991–92 as mild) and the low statistical significance (Vuille 1999) keep it below high.

## 2. Atacama Desert: winter–spring rain

**Sign, season, mechanism.** Rare but heavy rain on the Atacama coast comes from mid-latitude frontal systems that reach unusually far north in **winter of the developing year**. This happens when the SE Pacific anticyclone is weakened and blocking near ~90°W diverts the storm track equatorward. It is the same mechanism as central Chile's JJA(0) signal. Summer (DJF) rain in the interior and high Atacama comes instead from Altiplano/Amazon moisture and is associated with **La Niña**. That implies a dry-leaning interior summer in 2026–27.

**Key papers and quotes**
1. Vargas, G., Ortlieb, L. & Rutllant, J. (2000). Aluviones históricos en Antofagasta y su relación con eventos El Niño/Oscilación del Sur. *Revista Geológica de Chile* 27(2), 157–176. https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0716-02082000000200002&script=sci_arttext. (The page prints DOI 10.4067/S0716-02082000000200002, but it is **not registered** at Crossref or doi.org, so I cite the URL.)
   - "In all the cases, the rains occurred during the winter period of the developpement phase of El Niño (ENOS) events (very strong to moderate intensity), and were associated to frontal systems coming from higher latitudes" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: SciELO URL above]
   - Event dates (Table 3, from the text): 3 Jul 1925, 21 Aug 1930, 12 Jun and 24 Jul 1940, 24 May 1982, 27 Jul 1987, 18 Jun 1991. **All fall in May–August of year 0** [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
   - The rains "ocurren durante el invierno anterior al calentamiento estival de las aguas superficiales del mar ... y previo a la culminación del evento" (they occur in the winter before the summer coastal warming and before the event peaks) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
   - Caveat: "no todos los eventos El Niño han implicado años de elevada precipitación en la ciudad" (not every El Niño brought a wet year to the city) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]. Notably, **1997 (the strongest event of the century) does not appear** among the Antofagasta flood years of 1916–1999.
   - Interdecadal caveat: flood episodes clustered in 1925–1942 and after 1977, i.e., warm phases of the interdecadal Pacific [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]. This is relevant to the current strongly negative PDO.
2. Houston, J. (2006). Variability of precipitation in the Atacama Desert: its causes and hydrological impact. *Int. J. Climatol.* 26, 2181–2198. https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.1359
   - "winter rainfall tends to be higher during El Niño years, while heavy summer rainfall tends to be more common during La Niña." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
   - "Perennial rivers in the south tend to flood in summer, but as a result of melt from the previous years snowfall, especially during El Niño conditions" and "coastal areas on the other hand experience winter flooding of ephemeral rivers associated with El Niño." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
   - The abstract also says flood events "are generally less frequent than ENSO events, confirming the requirement for specific synoptic conditions" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
3. Bozkurt, D., Rondanelli, R., Garreaud, R. & Arriagada, A. (2016). Impact of warmer eastern tropical Pacific SST on the March 2015 Atacama floods. *Mon. Wea. Rev.* 144, 4441–4460. https://doi.org/10.1175/MWR-D-16-0041.1
   - "the warm SST anomaly over the eastern Pacific, favored by the onset of El Niño 2015/16, was instrumental to the extreme precipitation event" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. This was a cutoff-low event in **March of year 0**.
   - Counterpoint: Rondanelli et al. (2019, *GRL* 46, 3482–3491, https://doi.org/10.1029/2018GL081475) trace the same event to "the strongest Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO) on record" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
4. Vuille, M. & Ammann, C. (1997). Regional snowfall patterns in the high, arid Andes. *Climatic Change* 36, 413–423. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1005330802974. DOI verified; **abstract and full text not accessible**, so nothing is quoted. It is commonly cited for winter snowfall in the arid Andes during El Niño [SECONDARY/UNVERIFIED].

**Which winter/spring?** The verified literature supports **winter (May–Aug) of year 0**, i.e., **winter 2026, which is already past**. I found no peer-reviewed support for:
- (a) a Sep–Nov rainfall signal in the Atacama; the latest Antofagasta event month in 1916–1999 was August;
- (b) winter rain in the decay year (2027).

A plausible reading of "spring" is the **desierto florido** (flowering desert). That is a spring *ecological response* to year-0 winter rain, not a spring rainfall anomaly. I did not find a verified peer-reviewed source linking the bloom to El Niño, so this is [SECONDARY/UNVERIFIED].

One possibly still-relevant impact, supported only at abstract level (Houston 2006): **summer (DJF 2026–27) snowmelt floods on perennial rivers of the southern Atacama (Copiapó/Huasco)** if the 2026 winter left a large Andean snowpack. That depends on what winter 2026 actually delivered, which I have not checked.

**Recommendation: do not add an Atacama "wet" region to the forward-looking 2026–27 map.** If the map has a "what already happened in year 0" layer, winter 2026 frontal rain is defensible at **low-medium**. Rain is highly episodic and convective. Floods are rarer than El Niño events, 1997 produced no Antofagasta aluvión, and the negative PDO argues against. An optional low-confidence note could flag "dry-leaning interior/high Atacama summer" (La Niña-favoured summer rain) and the conditional snowmelt flood risk.

## 3. Central-southern Chile (30–40°S): the timing question

**Bottom line.** The canonical El Niño wet signal is in the **developing year**. It sits in **JJA(0) at 30–35°S** and moves south to **Oct–Nov(0) at 35–38°S**. Autumn has no significant signal. The literature does **not** support a wet Mar–Sep of the decay year.

**Key papers and quotes**
1. Montecinos, A. & Aceituno, P. (2003). Seasonality of the ENSO-related rainfall variability in central Chile and associated circulation anomalies. *J. Climate* 16, 281–296. https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0442(2003)016%3C0281:SOTERR%3E2.0.CO;2 [full text read: VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/clim/16/2/1520-0442_2003_016_0281_soterr_2.0.co_2.xml]
   - "During El Niño episodes there is a tendency for the occurrence of above-average precipitation between 30° and 35°S in winter [June–July–August (JJA)] and from 35° to 38°S in late spring [October–November (ON)], while rainfall deficit is typically observed from around 38° to 41°S during the following summer [January–February–March (JFM)], when El Niño reaches its maximum development."
   - "This study also concluded that rainfall in central Chile is not significantly related to ENSO in fall." (Citing Montecinos et al. 2000.)
   - Region A (30–35°S), JJA, r = 0.63 with Niño-3.4: "rainfall during the period 1958–98 was well above normal in all years when the Niño-3.4 (5°N–5°S, 120°–170°W) SST anomaly was larger than +1.0°C" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
   - Region B (35–38°S), Oct–Nov, r = 0.59: "a tendency for normal to above-normal late spring rainfall during warm episodes" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
   - Mechanism: blocking over the Amundsen–Bellingshausen Seas (~90°W) and a weakened SE Pacific anticyclone push the storm track north. The blocking "seem[s] to be part of a wave structure, particularly intense during spring" (PSA pattern).
2. Montecinos, A., Díaz, A. & Aceituno, P. (2000). Seasonal diagnostic and predictability of rainfall in subtropical South America based on tropical Pacific SST. *J. Climate* 13, 746–758. https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0442(2000)013%3C0746:SDAPOR%3E2.0.CO;2 [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/clim/13/4/1520-0442_2000_013_0746_sdapor_2.0.co_2.xml]
   - "From MA to MJ the patterns of rainfall anomalies do not show a consistent evolution in time, suggesting that the SST–rainfall relationship during fall is relatively weak."
   - The share of stations with significant correlations is "minimum during austral fall (2% in MA and 4% in AM)".
   - "During ON, most of the W1 and W2 cases concentrate ... in central–southern Chile (36°–39°S), where this period coincides with the end of the wet season." In ND the signal "weakens in Chile" and there is "a tendency for relatively dry conditions in central–southern Chile".
3. Rutllant, J. & Fuenzalida, H. (1991). Synoptic aspects of the central Chile rainfall variability associated with the Southern Oscillation. *Int. J. Climatol.* 11, 63–76. https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.3370110105
   - "Central Chile winter (June, July, August (JJA)) rainfall shows positive anomalies during the developing stage of warm events" and "Cold years, often immediately preceding or following a warm event, bring dry conditions in the study area" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
4. Aceituno, P. (1988). On the functioning of the Southern Oscillation in the South American sector. Part I: Surface climate. *Mon. Wea. Rev.* 116, 505–524. https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0493(1988)116%3C0505:OTFOTS%3E2.0.CO;2
   - "During the austral winter the tendency for anomalously abundant rainfall in central Chile during the negative SO phase appears related to an anomalously weak and northward displaced South Pacific subtropical high" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. Grimm et al. (2000) summarise Aceituno (1988) as finding significance "over Chile mainly during July–August and September–October" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY in Grimm et al.].
5. Grimm, A.M., Barros, V.R. & Doyle, M.E. (2000). Climate variability in southern South America associated with El Niño and La Niña events. *J. Climate* 13, 35–58. https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0442(2000)013%3C0035:CVISSA%3E2.0.CO;2 [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/clim/13/1/1520-0442_2000_013_0035_cvissa_2.0.co_2.xml]
   - "Central Chile, located in region 5, has a maximum in the first harmonic for the EN cycle in July (0), although the real maximum is in August (0)."
   - "In summer [December (0) to February (+)] ... There are no consistent wet anomalies over central Chile." In late summer (Feb–Apr (+)) anomalies "strengthen again ... in Chile, but not to the magnitude of spring (0)", and "In autumn (+) [April (+) to June (+)], normal conditions prevail over most of SSA."
   - This is the only verified hint of any year +1 wet tendency in Chile (Feb–Apr), and it is weak. The winter (+) wet signal Grimm et al. describe is for **southern Brazil**, not Chile.
6. Campos, D. & Rondanelli, R. (2023). ENSO-related precipitation variability in Central Chile: the role of large scale moisture transport. *JGR-Atmospheres* 128. https://doi.org/10.1029/2023JD038671
   - "an enhanced subtropical moisture transport during the warm ENSO phase leads to an increase in the frequency of atmospheric rivers ... moister, long-lived atmospheric rivers making landfall in Central Chile." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
7. Garreaud, R. et al. (2025). Hyperdroughts in central Chile: drivers, impacts, and projections. *HESS* 29, 5347–5369. https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-29-5347-2025 [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/5347/2025/hess-29-5347-2025.pdf]
   - "interannual variations of RPI are significantly correlated with the Nino3.4 index (r = +0.66), but HDs occur under mostly neutral ENSO conditions"
   - Hyperdrought years: 1924, 1968, **1998**, 2019, 2021. On 1998: "there was a sharp transition from the strong El Niño of 1997 to La Niña conditions, but the latter became established at the end of winter 1998, making it difficult to relate to 1998 HD entirely." The May 1998 circulation had an SLP dipole with "the 'wrong' polarity in connection with the demise of the 1997 El Niño event".

**Decay-year behaviour (the key question), computed from a verified table.** I cross-referenced Garreaud et al. (2017, *HESS*, Table 2) with the strong-event list defined above. Table 2 lists the years 1915–2015 when the central Chile regional precipitation index (RPI; annual, 7 stations 32–37°S, % of 1970–2000 mean) fell below 75%.
- **Developing years (year 0):** 1 of 8 was a drought year: **2015** (RPI = 75.00%). The other seven were not: 1957, 1965, 1972, 1982, 1991, 1997, 2009.
- **Decay years (year +1):** 3 of 7 were drought years: **1973** (67%), **1998** (32%, the second driest since 1915), **2010** (72%). Not drought: 1958, 1966, 1983, 1992.
- **Base rate:** 24 drought years in 1915–2009, about 25%.
- Caveats: n is tiny, RPI is annual (not MAM–JJA), and "not a drought" does not mean "wet". Even so, it points the opposite way from a "wetter Mar–Sep of the decay year" call. Source: [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/21/6307/2017/hess-21-6307-2017.pdf]. The event classification is my own, from the local ONI file.
- ONI in JJA(+1) was −0.63 (1998), −0.99 (1973), −0.98 (2010), −0.19 (2016) and +0.06 (2024). The JJA Niño-3.4 forcing that drives the winter signal is usually gone or reversed by the decay-year winter.

**2015 winter (a documented underperformance).** Documented in peer-reviewed sources:
- Garreaud et al. (2017, *HESS* 21, 6307–6327, https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-21-6307-2017) treat 2015 as the sixth megadrought year (RPI 75%). They write that "a 4-year drought chain is unlikely, setting aside the occurrence of a fifth dry year during a strong El Niño event." [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
- Garreaud et al. (2020; online 2019, *IJC* 40, 421–439, https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.6219): "the MD has occurred mostly under ENSO‐neutral conditions, except for the winters of 2010 (La Niña) and 2015 (strong El Niño)" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. They attribute the dipole to SW Pacific warming plus a positive SAM trend.
- JJA 2015 ONI was +1.44. That breaks the "all years with Niño-3.4 > +1.0°C were wet" rule of Montecinos & Aceituno (1958–98).

**2023 winter.** Documented:
- Krogh, S.A., Garreaud, R. et al. (2026, *J. Hydrometeorol.*, https://doi.org/10.1175/JHM-D-25-0149.1): "In June 2023, an extreme hydrometeorological event caused widespread flooding across Central Chile (34–39°S), abruptly interrupting a 13-year dry period. ... A Category 4 Zonal Atmospheric River persisted for 4 days ... rainfall accumulations of up to 600 mm, and the freezing level above 3,000 m ASL." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. The abstract does not attribute the event to El Niño. JJA 2023 ONI was +1.00.
- The **August 2023** event and any El Niño attribution are documented only in an EGU conference abstract, CR2 blog posts, NASA Earth Observatory and Wikipedia [SECONDARY/UNVERIFIED].

**Other strong years.**
- 1965 and 1972: JJA ONI +1.10 and +1.00. Both fall within the Montecinos & Aceituno 1958–98 "all above normal when Niño-3.4 > +1.0" sample, although whether their Niño-3.4 exceeded the threshold depends on the dataset. Neither is a drought year in HESS Table 2.
- 1982 and 1997: in the 1958–98 sample and not drought years. I did not find a verified quote giving their rainfall totals.
- 1957: before the Montecinos & Aceituno sample and not a drought year in Table 2.

**Winter 2026 (already happened, JJA ONI +1.80).** I did not verify what central Chile actually received. Check DMC/CR2 station data before mentioning it; given the 2015 failure it should not be assumed.

**Recommendation.**
- Replace "wetter Mar–Sep" with **"wetter than normal Oct–Nov 2026, ~35–38°S (Maule, Ñuble, Biobío, northern Araucanía)"** at **medium** confidence. That is the one remaining year-0 window, and it is the season when the PSA wave train is strongest. The limits: ON is the tail of the wet season, so absolute amounts are modest; r = 0.59 is the weaker of the two correlations; and the megadrought-era dipole and positive SAM trend can override it, as in 2015.
- **Do not map wet Mar–Sep 2027.** The best-supported statement for the decay-year autumn is "no significant ENSO signal". For winter 2027, the 1973/1998/2010 precedents suggest dry risk if La Niña develops.
- JJA 2026 (30–35°S) was the canonical season (historically medium-high to high), but it is past.

## 4. Southern Chile / western Patagonia (40–50°S): dry summer

**Sign, season, mechanism.** Dry in the **summer of the mature phase, JFM(+1) = Jan–Mar 2027**, most robustly at **38–41°S**. More frequent ridging near the southern tip of South America reinforces the southern edge of the subtropical anticyclone and weakens the westerlies that deliver orographic rain.

**Key papers and quotes**
1. Montecinos & Aceituno (2003), cited above [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]:
   - "there is a significant tendency for the occurrence of relatively dry (wet) conditions along the southern part of central Chile (38°–41°S) during summers coinciding with El Niño (La Niña) episodes ... (correlation of −0.44 ...). Rainfall remains in the first tercile of the distribution in most summers with SST anomalies above +1.0°C"
   - Caveat, same paper: "During summer the influence of ENSO on rainfall is considerably weaker"; the circulation anomaly "seems weakly modulated by ENSO".
2. Montecinos et al. (2000), cited above: the summer and early-fall dry tendency (JF, FM) in central–southern Chile "does not have a counterpart of opposite rainfall anomalies during La Niña". The longer 1919–89 check found "the stronger tendency for a rainfall deficit in Chile during JF" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY].
3. Schneider, C. & Gies, D. (2004). Effects of El Niño–southern oscillation on southernmost South America precipitation at 53°S revealed from NCEP–NCAR reanalyses and weather station data. *Int. J. Climatol.* 24, 1057–1076. https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.1057
   - Positive SOI–precipitation correlation (r = 0.4) "west of the Patagonian coastline between 45 and 55°S based on annually averaged data. Precipitation decreases by about 15% during strong El Niños" and "The generally weak positive correlation between precipitation and SOI was also found" in station data [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
4. Aravena, J.C. & Luckman, B.H. (2009; online 2008). Spatio-temporal rainfall patterns in Southern South America. *Int. J. Climatol.* 29, 2106–2120. https://doi.org/10.1002/joc.1761
   - "Regional annual rainfall appears to be strongly influenced by Antarctic circulation modes (Antarctic Oscillation Index), whereas the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) influence on rainfall variability is less evident." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
5. Garreaud (2009), cited above: "Farther south, over the Austral Andes, there is no clear ENSO signal in precipitation or temperature. In contrast, the Southern Annual Mode (SAM ...) ... appears to modulate the air temperature over the southern tip of South America" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]
6. Garreaud, R. (2018). Record-breaking climate anomalies lead to severe drought and environmental disruption in western Patagonia in 2016. *Clim. Res.* 74, 217–229. https://doi.org/10.3354/cr01505
   - Western Patagonia "experienced its most severe drought during the summer and fall of 2016. Along with precipitation deficits larger than 50%" and "The local climate anomalies were in turn related to the concomitant strong El Nino (through atmospheric teleconnections) and, to a lesser extent, anthropogenic climate change mediated by the positive polarity of the Southern Annular Mode (SAM)" [ABSTRACT-ONLY]
7. León-Muñoz, J., Urbina, M.A., Garreaud, R. & Iriarte, J.L. (2018). Hydroclimatic conditions trigger record harmful algal bloom in western Patagonia (summer 2016). *Sci. Rep.* 8, 1330. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-19461-4 [VERIFIED-PRIMARY: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19461-4]
   - "When ENSO is in its warm phase (El Niño condition) during the austral summer, SLP increases and the westerlies weaken off the southern tip of the continent, leading to drier than average conditions in western Patagonia"
   - Key caveat: "The fact that both modes were in their positive state during the summer of 2016 is surprising, considering that El Niño conditions favour the negative phase of SAM"
   - The HAB "killed nearly 12% of the Chilean salmon production" (abstract). This is a concrete impact example for the map text.
8. L'Heureux, M.L. & Thompson, D.W.J. (2006). Observed relationships between the El Niño–Southern Oscillation and the extratropical zonal-mean circulation. *J. Climate* 19, 276–287. https://doi.org/10.1175/JCLI3617.1
   - "During the austral summer, roughly 25% of the temporal variability in the SAM is linearly related to fluctuations in the ENSO cycle." [ABSTRACT-ONLY]. El Niño favours negative SAM, which tends to **strengthen** westerlies near 40–50°S and so works against the dry signal.
9. Garreaud, R.D., Vuille, M., Compagnucci, R. & Marengo, J. (2009). Present-day South American climate. *Palaeo3* 281, 180–195. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.palaeo.2007.10.032. DOI verified; **not accessed**, so nothing is quoted.

**Behaviour in strong events.**
- **2015–16:** extreme dry JFM and autumn 2016 (above), but co-forced by record-high SAM.
- **1982–83, 1997–98:** I found **no verified event-specific documentation** for western Patagonia.
- **Positive IOD analogue (single case):** Garreaud et al. (2025, *HESS*) describe winter 2019. The late-winter SLP pattern "persisted until summer, expanding the extremely dry conditions from central Chile to Patagonia, and coincided with extreme values of the Indian Ocean Dipole, suggesting a teleconnection with that remote area" [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]. With a positive IOD developing in 2026, this is worth noting, but it is one case.

**Recommendation.** Relabel as **"drier than normal Jan–Mar 2027, ~38–42°S (Araucanía, Los Ríos, Los Lagos, Chiloé)"** at **medium**. Keep **42–50°S (Aysén, western Patagonia proper)** at **low**. There, several sources describe a weak or unclear ENSO signal, the best documented case (2016) needed a record positive SAM, and El Niño's own tendency toward negative SAM works against drying. A DJF window is acceptable, but the verified literature specifies JFM.

## Cross-cutting caveats for 2026–27

- **Negative PDO.** For the Atacama, flood episodes clustered in warm interdecadal phases (Vargas et al. 2000) [VERIFIED-PRIMARY]. For the Altiplano, the evidence conflicts (see §1). For central Chile, I did not verify any PDO-conditioning study. The 1960s–early-1970s events (1965, 1972) happened in a cool-PDO era and were not drought years, which weakly argues against a strong PDO veto.
- **SAM trend and the megadrought dipole.** Garreaud et al. (2020) and the 2015 case show that the SW Pacific warming dipole plus a positive SAM trend can override the El Niño winter signal in central Chile. The same SAM trend amplified the 2016 Patagonian drought.
- **Positive IOD.** Only the single 2019 case above links extreme positive IOD to dry central Chile and Patagonia in spring and summer. It adds some dry risk for Oct 2026–Mar 2027 south of ~35°S. This partly **opposes** the Oct–Nov 35–38°S wet signal. I did not find a verified multi-event study on this.
